This is the World Health Organization's ASRS v1.1, developed with the WHO Composite International Diagnostic Interview working group. It's the instrument a GP or psychiatrist is most likely to hand you at a first appointment. It's free to use and reproduce.

Part A

The screener · items 1–6

These six questions carry the screening decision. Grey cells are the thresholds — they sit at a different frequency for different questions, because some symptoms are more discriminating than others. Four or more marks inside grey is the positive screen.

Part B

Symptom picture · items 7–18

Part B has no official cutoff. It exists to describe the shape of the difficulty — which is what a clinician needs in order to tell inattentive, hyperactive-impulsive, and combined presentations apart.

Context

Not part of the ASRS

Symptom frequency alone never makes an ADHD diagnosis. DSM-5 also requires early onset, more than one setting, real impairment, and no better explanation. These questions are the ones that most often change a clinician's mind — in both directions.

    About the instrument. ASRS v1.1 questions © World Health Organization; the screener was developed by Kessler and colleagues with the WHO and is free to reproduce. Part A's six items screen positive at four or more threshold marks, with roughly 68% sensitivity and 99% specificity in the general population — meaning a positive result is worth taking seriously, and a negative result does not rule ADHD out, particularly for inattentive presentations, women, and people who have built strong coping structures.

    What a screener can't see. Chronic short sleep, untreated anxiety or depression, thyroid problems, iron deficiency, long COVID, grief, and sustained overload all produce this exact symptom profile. That is not a reason to dismiss a high score — it's the reason the assessment belongs with a clinician who can take a history and test the alternatives.
